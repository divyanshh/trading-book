The finding

₹20 L returned +24.0% in under six months, against a smallcap index that did much the same — with a Sharpe of 1.66.

Total P&L ₹4,80,677 on a fixed ₹20 L base over 116 sessions, ₹4,77,570 of it realised. Sharpe 1.66, Sortino 2.38, Calmar 6.35 — above 1 is good and above 2 is strong, so the book was well paid for its risk. It beat holding government bonds by ₹4,16,643 and beat the Nifty 50 (+2.6%) and Nifty 500 by a wide margin.

Against the index it actually trades, it delivered beta, not alpha. Beta to Nifty Smallcap 100 is 1.017 with t = 8.4 — a very solid measurement — while alpha is +1.19% with t = 0.04, which is zero. R² of 0.39 is the highest of any benchmark here. Read plainly: the return came from being fully exposed to smallcaps through a smallcap rally, executed with good risk control. That is a real achievement; it is not yet evidence of stock-picking edge.

Source, and a correction. Every figure here now comes from the published daily series behind the site’s own performance chart — 2,373 broker fills across the full Zerodha account, reconciled to dated exports. Earlier versions of this page reconstructed a curve from the service’s Trade table, which is a narrower subset, and reported +2.38%, then +30.62%, then +19.97% as capital and phantom-position errors were found. All three should be discarded. The service database is not the performance record; the fill replay is.
This does not agree with the 14 October risk page, and the difference is the capital base. That page reports +9.38% total return, Sharpe 1.18 and beta 0.372 against Nifty Smallcap 100 over a slightly different window (13 Apr – 13 Oct). Its return basis implies a denominator roughly 2.5× the ₹20 L used here and on the site’s own performance chart — which is why its beta is about a third of this one and its return about two-fifths. Returns and beta both scale inversely with assumed capital, so these are the same book measured against different bases rather than a disagreement about what happened. ₹20 L is the base the owner confirms and the one the headline chart uses.

The worst one is still open at the end of the window: the book peaked at ₹27.27 L on 31 August and finished 9.03% below that high. Four falls between 3.9% and 9.6% in under six months is the real texture of this book — the +24% headline was not a smooth line.

What each of these actually means

Plain English, our number, and whether it is good or bad.

Standard deviation (volatility)26.6% a year

How much the book’s value jumps around day to day, annualised. Pure movement — it does not care whether the movement is up or down.

High, but so is what it tracks. This is a concentrated smallcap book, and 26.6% against the index’s own volatility is the price of the return rather than a fault.

Sharpe ratio1.66

Return above the risk-free rate, divided by volatility. “How much reward did each unit of wobble buy?” Above 1 is good, above 2 is strong, below 0 means a fixed deposit beat you.

Good, close to strong. 1.66 is a number most funds would be pleased with. The book earned about ₹4.17 L more than government bonds would have paid on the same ₹20 L.

Sortino ratio2.38

Same as Sharpe, but only counts downward wobble as risk — being punished for upside volatility is unfair, so this is the fairer version.

Strong. Above 2, and well above the Sharpe — most of the volatility was upside. The book moved hard in the right direction more often than the wrong one.

Maximum drawdown−9.56%

The most the account fell from its own high. Not a hypothetical entry at the top — this is money that was on the screen and then was not. You lived through it.

Mark-to-market, not realised. On booked P&L alone the worst looks like −10.46%, but the account gained ₹1.47 L across those dates — losers cut, winners still running. Realised-only drawdown misleads for a book built this way; this is the real figure.

Calmar ratio6.35

Annual return divided by the worst drawdown. “How much return per unit of worst-case pain?” Above 1 is respectable, above 3 is excellent.

Excellent. 6.35 is a strong ratio of return to worst-case pain. Mild caution: Calmar rests on a single worst episode, and six months holds few of them.

Beta1.02 vs smallcap

How much the book moves when the market moves. 1.0 means it tracks the index; 0.5 means half as much; 0 means it ignores the index entirely.

Essentially 1.0 — the book IS smallcap exposure. t = 8.4, the most solidly measured number on this page. It moved one-for-one with the index it trades.

Alpha (Jensen’s)+1.2% a year

The return left after accounting for whatever the market handed you for free. Positive alpha is the part you can claim as skill — the number fund managers are judged on.

Zero. t = 0.04 against the 1.96 needed. Once the smallcap move is accounted for, nothing is left over that this sample can measure. The +24% is beta well executed, not proven selection skill — which is a real result, just not the one the word alpha claims.

t-statistic0.04 on alpha

How confident we can be that a number is not luck. |t| above 1.96 means 95% confident it is real; below that you cannot distinguish it from random.

The honest brake on the alpha above. The beta carries t = 8.4, so the exposure is a measured fact; the skill is not. More sessions is the only cure.

R²0.39 vs smallcap

What share of the book’s movement is explained by the index. 1.0 means it is the index; 0 means it does its own thing.

39% came from the smallcap index, 61% from the specific stocks picked — the highest R² of any benchmark here, which confirms smallcap is the right yardstick for this book.

Tracking error20.8% vs smallcap

How far the book’s returns drift from the benchmark’s, annualised. Low means you hug the index; high means you go your own way.

High — nothing like an index fund. The drift was roughly neutral in net effect: wide swings either side of the index that came out about even.

Information ratio0.09 vs smallcap

Return earned above the benchmark, per unit of how far you strayed from it. Above 0.5 is good, above 1 is excellent.

Essentially zero. Straying from the smallcap index neither helped nor hurt. Against Nifty 500 (1.45) and Nifty 50 (1.80) it looks strong, but those are not what this book trades.

What went right

It was well paid for its risk. Sharpe 1.66 and Sortino 2.38 are numbers most funds would be happy to show, and the book beat government bonds by ₹4,16,643 on ₹20 L. Calmar 6.35. 62% of sessions were positive.

The drawdown behaved too: worst −9.56%, recovered in 33 days, and every episode bar the last one was recovered inside the window.

What it did not do is add measurable value over its own index. That is the one thing six months of data says clearly, and it is worth separating from the headline: executing smallcap beta well is a real achievement, and it is not the same claim as alpha.

Method, and what to distrust

Measured, not reconstructed. The daily series is the one published behind the site’s performance chart: 2,373 broker fills across the full Zerodha account, marked to each day’s close, with profit moved from unrealised to realised as positions are sold. Realised P&L reconciles to dated Zerodha exports.

Full account, not strategy-only. The series covers the whole equity account rather than the narrower algo subset, so these figures describe the account’s performance, not the strategy’s in isolation.

Half a year is a short sample. 115 return observations. A Sharpe estimated over this window carries a wide confidence interval, and square-root-of-time annualisation assumes returns are serially independent, which trend-following returns often are not. Treat the annualised figures as scaling, not forecasts.

Costs. P&L is before brokerage, STT and slippage. Across 2,373 fills that is a real drag not reflected above, so every return figure here is optimistic by some margin.

Where to buy a large cap

The daily report has listed every Nifty 50 member above its 50-day average, with its distance to the 20, the 10 and its own 52-week high. It reports. It gates nothing, and nothing in this service had ever measured whether any of those readings is a better moment to buy than any other. This is that measurement — seven entry points, five stops, seven ways of selling and the two preconditions you asked for, replayed together over every index member we can price — set beside what the published literature actually claims, and what sample size it claims it on.

The verdict, before the detail

Nothing here earns a gate, and I am proposing none. Over 715 sessions from January 2024 to yesterday, every one of the 980 combinations lost money, while simply buying all 48 priceable index members and holding them returned +0.4%. Worse than that: the control — buy on any session at all — had the best return per trade of any entry rule tested. Not one of the six chart conditions picked a better moment than entering at random.

Your two preconditions do move the book, and not for a reason worth owning. Requiring positive market breadth and a positive sector cuts the signals by 90%, and a book that takes a tenth as many trades loses a tenth as much: mean book return improves from −14.8% ungated to −6.6% with both conditions on. Per trade it gets worse — −0.47R against −0.31R. And a permutation that keeps the gate's run lengths and its share of positive days but destroys its alignment with what happened next beat the real gate 294 times in 300 (p = 0.980). The gate's content here is that it trades less, not that it trades better.

And the reason given for wanting large caps does not hold. You said small caps are not running while large caps are. On our own closes, on the same sessions, non-index names returned +15.4% in calendar 2026 against the index members' +7.1%, with a similar share of each cohort up. Small and mid caps are running; large caps are the laggard. If our small-cap book has not kept up, that is a selection problem, and it does not get fixed by rotating into the cohort that returned less.

Two effects did survive everything: wider stops lose less, monotonically, and moving-average trails are the worst exits on book return — they cut the loser and the winner alike. Both run against rules we currently use.

1 · What is actually published, and how thin it is

The brief was to read the literature before measuring anything, so this section is what the web actually carries on where a large cap should be bought, stopped and sold — with the sample size each source reports, where it reports one. The short version is that almost nothing here is strong enough to act on, and the two results closest to our own question point the opposite way to the premise.

Where to buy

The pullback-to-a-moving-average entry is the most written-about and the worst evidenced. Investing Paths tested a 50-EMA pullback behind a 200-day trend filter across 22 US large caps and sector ETFs: 173 trades, a 34.1% win rate, and it beat buy-and-hold in zero of the 22 names. The authors blame the fixed 2R target rather than the entry. Journal Plus describes the 50-day as a secondary buy point inside a 15–25% pullback band and reports no backtest at all.

The breakout entry comes from O'Neil's school, and what it says is specific: buy the first close above the pivot on volume at least 40% above the 50-day average, never chase more than 5% past the pivot, and cut every loss at 7–8% (The Right Trader, IBD on alternative buy points). The pocket-pivot variant requires the stock to have been respecting its 50-day throughout the advance (Morales & Kacher). None of these carries a published trade count.

The premise disagrees with its own source

CAN SLIM is not a large-cap method. Its own description and Money Crashers both state it is explicitly not looking for older large-capitalisation companies. There is no large-cap-specific O'Neil buy point to import — the pivot and the 7–8% stop are conventions borrowed across, not results measured on index members.

The 52-week-high entry has the only genuinely academic backing here, and it does not survive the trip to India. George & Hwang (2004) is the original US result; a partial replication on the top 1,500 NYSE names 1998–2007 supports it. But Barroso & Wang confine the effect to small stocks, and the one study that covers our actual universe — Mandhyan, 431 Indian stocks 2015–2023 — finds every momentum variant delivers a statistically insignificant winner-minus-loser spread among big caps specifically. The 52-week-high signal worked in Indian mid-caps (0.987% a month against 0.740% for standard momentum); the full-universe spread was 0.726% at t = 1.874, which is marginal.

Every public test of a moving-average rule on Indian index names found the same shape: lower return than buy-and-hold, with a smaller drawdown. That is a consistent finding, and it is a finding about a trend filter, not about an entry point.

Stops and profit-booking

There is no rigorous head-to-head of ATR against fixed percentage stops in the public record — which is itself the finding. MQL5 asserts ATR superiority citing no studies. A TradingView analysis cites Kestner (2003) for a 28% Sharpe improvement across 15 futures markets, which could not be verified. Quantified Trader reports an ATR trailing stop across 20 US indices and ETFs averaging Sharpe 0.57 on a 169.4% return and a −25.7% maximum drawdown — indices, not single names.

On trailing against targets, StockCharts' SystemTrader found that on an S&P 1500 crossover system returning 9.27% a year, a profit target kept 8.99% while a trailing stop dropped it below 7% — but the trailing stop tested was a Chandelier Exit, not a moving average, so it does not settle our question either. Finer Market Points is the clearest statement of the practitioner rule we already use — the 10- or 20-day average as the trail, the 20-day for more volatile names, usually combined with a prior swing low — and it is entirely anecdotal.

Partial booking is the one place the sources agree. Enlightened Stock Trading tested it on a trend system with MAR 0.52 and found every partial profit-taking variant lowered both returns and risk-adjusted performance. ActivTrades and Traders Second Brain describe the mechanism: partials raise the win rate and lower the average profit per trade. That is a comfort trade, not an edge.

Two methodological warnings taken into the measurement below

Financial Edge: implement every position with a one-day lag or the test reads a price the decision could not have been made at. Every entry below fills at the next session's open, never the signal close.

Setup4Alpha: tightening a stop also changes cash drag, turnover, position count and which signals had room, so a plain backtest blends the exit effect with all four. That is why every cell below is also sequenced through a real book with slots and capital, not counted as free trades.

On sample size, Fortraders calls anything under 100 trades “noise dressed up as statistics” and asks for 200 across regimes; Trade-Ideas agrees a small sample is a pilot, not proof. Most of the sources quoted on this page fail that bar.

2 · What the books claim, and what independent tests found

He asked for books as well as the internet. Five are worth the time for this question; all five are read here for the precondition, because that is the part of his instruction with the most published evidence behind it, and it is not the part most of these books are famous for.

The two that are actually about a precondition

Mebane Faber, A Quantitative Approach to Tactical Asset Allocation (2007, updated 2013). The rule is one line: hold the asset while it is above its ten-month average, otherwise hold cash. It is the most-tested market-state filter in print, and the honest reading of the tests is mixed in a specific, useful way. An independent replay on SPY counts 23 completed trades in 33 years and finds the whole advantage resting on three exits across two bear markets — and that randomising the entry dates reproduced much of the protection, which says the benefit comes from a slow average sitting out long declines rather than from timing anything. The same test reports the filter has cost return since 2007: 8.2% a year against SPY's 10.9%. CXO Advisory adds the data-snooping objection to the ten-month choice; Faber concedes it himself and argues every length from six to twelve months behaves the same, which is the right answer to that objection and also an admission that the parameter carries no information. His own ten-year revisit argues it held up out of sample.

Gary Antonacci, Dual Momentum Investing (2014). Two filters: pick the stronger of two assets (relative), and hold neither unless it is beating treasury bills (absolute). The absolute leg is a precondition in exactly his sense. Independent work since publication is consistently weaker than the book: Petit's SSRN replication over 1971–2026 finds it underperformed a passive index by 4.8 points a year since 2010, with its deepest fifty-five-year drawdown occurring in 2021–23; an out-of-sample replication reproduces the book's own decomposition table and finds the absolute-momentum leg failing to reduce drawdown, because a monthly signal cannot exit a fast crash; a post-publication test reports it lagging a plain 60/40 with the drawdown edge mostly a 2008 story. Against that, a 2008–2026 decomposition concludes the opposite about which part earns its keep: the absolute-momentum gate did the work and the relative leg cost 1.4 to 3.2 points a year at every lookback tested. Antonacci's own reply denies degradation.

Read together these say something narrow and worth having: a market-state precondition is the best-supported single idea in this literature, its support is for drawdown rather than return, and it reliably costs return when the market goes up anyway. None of those tests is on Indian equities, on single stocks, or on a daily signal.

The three that are about where to buy

Stan Weinstein, Secrets for Profiting in Bull and Bear Markets (1988). Four stages around a 30-week average; buy Stage 2 breakouts only. It is the most large-cap-applicable rule in print and the evidence for it is the thinnest here: searching for a rigorous test returns one blog claiming it works on a 150- or 200-day average with no universe, no trade count and no drawdown, an MQL5 expert advisor coded for forex, and otherwise chart walkthroughs. No published trade count exists for it. Our own grid tests the daily analogue — trend, close above the 50-day — on 10,187 signals.

Andreas Clenow, Stocks on the Move (2015). Rank the S&P 500 by 90-day regression slope × R², buy only while the index is above its 200-day average, sell on falling out of the ranking or below the 100-day. Note that it carries a precondition too. A 2006–2025 retest finds every version did worse than SPY over the full twenty years, though the rules beat the market over the book's own 2006–2014 window — the standard shape of a rule published at the end of the period that produced it. An independent implementation got ~9%/yr against SPY's 12%, with an 11% maximum drawdown against the index's deeper one. A QuantConnect test over 2009–2024 reports 14.81% against 8.86% but notes buy-and-hold beat it over the same period.

Gray & Vogel, Quantitative Momentum (2016), and O'Neil. Gray and Vogel's tests run on a 500-stock universe with a six-month lookback, gross of fees, and their own summary notes the gain from equal weighting "benefiting from diversification and the small-cap effect" — which is the same caveat the academic 52-week-high literature carries, and it points away from large caps rather than towards them. O'Neil's often-quoted decomposition — about 37% of a stock's move from its industry group and a further 12% from its sector — is the stated reason a sector precondition should exist at all. Chartmill traces it to O'Neil's own IBD research and cautions that the exact share varies by method while the direction does not, citing Roll (1992) and Heston & Rouwenhorst (1994) behind the industry effect. There is no primary study published with those two numbers; they come from the book. Separately, CAN SLIM is explicitly not a large-cap method.

And on breadth specifically

A direct search for a measured test of breadth as an entry filter returns descriptions and dashboards, not results: what the indicator is, a regime-signal treatment whose finding is about tomorrow's range rather than entry returns, and guides on reading it. Nobody appears to have published a like-for-like comparison of the same entries with and without a breadth threshold, reporting trade counts. That is the gap this page fills on our own data.

The three decisions, and why they are tested together

A tight stop with a far target is not a strategy, it is two rules that disagree. So the unit here is the whole rule — when you are allowed to buy at all, where you buy, where you are wrong, where you sell — and every one of the 980 combinations is replayed over the same sessions, the same fifty names and the same capital.

The four things that stop a cell meaning nothing

  • The population. What fraction of available symbol-sessions the entry even fires on. A rule that fires twice a year cannot fill a book; a rule that fires every day is not a rule. This is printed first because it reframes every win rate that follows.
  • The main effect. Each factor level's marginal mean across every combination of the other two. A single cell can win on noise. A level that wins across thirty-five cells is harder to get by accident.
  • The within-period split. The same effects on each half of the window measured alone. A cross-section is not evidence until it survives this. A level that only works in one half worked on one market, not on a mechanism.
  • The permutation. Shuffling which session each signal was read on, holding the symbol and the signal count fixed. That keeps the sample size, the names and the market identical, and destroys only the claim under test — that the rule picked the moment. The precondition is permuted the same way, by reordering the sessions rather than the flags, so each sector keeps its run lengths, its share of positive days and its correlation with the index, and only the alignment with what the market did next is broken.
  • The control. any-session is in the grid for the same reason a placebo arm is in a trial. A rule that cannot beat buying on no condition whatsoever is not a buy rule, however good its own numbers look.
  • The benchmark. Buying all 48 priceable members equally at the first measurable open and holding to the end. Without it, “every cell lost money” could be a verdict on the window rather than on the rules.
Sequenced against the book, not counted as free trades

Every cell is also run through a ₹60 lakh pool with twelve slots, rupee-risk sizing and a deploy cap. Without that, a rule firing forty signals a session is credited with forty positions it could never have funded, and the ranking inverts against one that fires twice. When more signals fire than there are slots, the book takes the names nearest their own 52-week high first — the order the daily report already prints them in, so the replay fills what a reader of that section would actually have reached for.

Drawdown is marked to market, with open positions valued at every session's close. A realised-only drawdown falls hardest exactly when the strategy is working, which makes it worse than useless.

What the replay cannot pay: a stop is filled at the lower of the stop and that session's open, because a broker cannot execute above the whole session's range — a replay that lets it manufactures its own edge on every gap down. Every exit carries 0.5% slippage. Every fill is at the next session's open, so no decision reads a price it could not have been made at. A position that neither stops nor triggers its exit is closed at the market after 60 sessions.

The breadth condition behaves exactly as designed: it halves the signals, because it is positive half the time. The sector condition does not — it removes 86% of them, far more than a roughly even condition should, and the reason is the caveat that governs every gated number on this page.

Read the sector column as a 13-name filter, not as a sector condition

A sector line needs at least eight priced members before it is drawn at all, and only 13 of the 48 index members belong to a sector that clears that floor. For the other 35 the sector is ungradeable, and ungradeable is treated as a failure, never as a pass — waving it through would credit the condition with trades it would never have allowed. That is the right default and it has a consequence: the sector and both columns are mostly selecting which names have a measurable sector, not which sectors are strong. Any book return they show is partly a bet on thirteen specific companies. It is the single biggest reason not to act on the gated cells below, and it is fixable — by grading a Nifty-50 name against a broader sector definition — but it is not fixed today.

Taken at face value, the best cell made +7.5% against the benchmark's +0.4%, at a −9.5% drawdown against its −13.1%. That is the result a backtest gets published on. Three things stop it being one here.

  • It is not a buy rule. Every cell in the top twenty-five is any-session. The thing being measured is “buy something when the conditions are on”, and the chart conditions you actually asked about — the pullback, the reclaim, the 52-week high — are all absent because all of them made it worse.
  • The book made money while the average trade lost. −0.21R mean on a +7.5% book is sequencing, not edge: with 90% of signals removed, twelve slots and a ₹5 lakh deploy cap, very little capital is ever at work, and the few trades that do fill carry the result. 156 trades over 715 sessions is one position opened every four and a half sessions.
  • Thirteen names. Every sector and both row inherits the gradeability problem above.

6 · Main effects

A single cell can win on noise. A factor level averaged over every combination of the other three is harder to get by accident, and it is the only part of a grid worth reading first.

Both columns move, and they move in opposite directions. The gate is the strongest single factor on book return — 8.2 points between none and both, bigger than the stop, the exit or the entry — and it is the worst factor on return per trade, costing 0.16R. One number says the condition works; the other says each trade it admits is worse than the trades it rejected.

Both are true, and the reconciliation is uninteresting: a precondition that removes 90% of signals produces a book that is mostly in cash. Less capital at risk in a flat-to-down window loses less. It is the same result you would get from halving the slot count, and it is not a reason to believe the condition saw anything.

Where to buy — the question you asked

This is the finding that matters most and it is the one nobody wants. Five of the six chart conditions returned less per trade than entering at random, and the sixth, dip-under-fast, beats the control by 0.019R, which the permutation below cannot distinguish from chance. The two conditions that look most like what we actually trade — proximity to a 52-week high, and a fresh 52-week high — are the two worst things on the page, at −0.243R and −0.386R.

On book return rather than per-trade return the order shifts a little, because the rarer entries fill fewer slots: any-session −8.6%, dip-under-fast −8.7%, pullback20 −9.3%, reclaim10 −9.5%, trend −10.7%, breakout52 −11.8%, near-high −14.3%. The control is still first.

Wider is better, monotonically, on return and on drawdown at the same time. This is the most stable thing in the whole measurement: it held in the ungated grid, it holds in the gated one, and it holds on both halves of the window. A 12% stop lost a third of what a 5% stop lost and drew down a third as deep — because a tight stop on a large cap is not protection, it is a tax. The name comes back and you are not in it, and you have paid 0.5% slippage to find that out.

Note what that does not say. It does not say “widen our stops”. Wider stops on fixed rupee risk mean smaller positions, which is the trade-off the book already makes, and it does not say anything about small caps, where the structural stop is drawn from the chart and not from a percentage. It says that on Nifty-50 names, in this window, a stop tight enough to be hit by ordinary noise cost more than it saved.

You asked specifically about trailing on the 20-day. The moving-average trails are the three worst exits on book return and the three best on return per trade, and the gap between those two facts is the whole answer. A fast trail exits early and often: it keeps each individual loss small, which flatters mean R, and it also exits the winners, which is what the book actually lives on. The faster the average, the stronger the effect — the 10-day is both the least-bad per trade (−0.16R) and the worst for the book (−13.9%).

On these names, in this window, taking half at 2R and trailing the remainder was the least bad of the seven, and doing nothing at all but holding to the stop was second. Neither made money.

The permutation

p = 0.980

The gate's sessions were reordered 300 times — the whole session index shuffled, so every sector keeps its run lengths, its share of positive days and its correlation with the market reading intact, and the only thing destroyed is the alignment between a positive reading and what happened next. The real gate admitted 19,764 of 199,258 trades at −0.053R against ungated. A random reordering matched or beat that 294 times in 300.

Read it the right way round. This does not say the gate is harmful; the edge being tested is negative, so a high p-value here means a reshuffled gate is usually less bad than the real one. What it says is that there is no timing information in the real condition that a pattern with the same shape and no knowledge of the future does not also have. Whatever the gate did to the book, it did not do by knowing when to buy.

The separate entry permutation, run on the best-looking entry comparison in the grid, says the same thing more quietly: dip-under-fast's +0.050R edge over trend at an 8% stop and a 20-day trail was matched or beaten by chance 64 times in 300, p = 0.216. Not significant at any threshold worth naming.

8 · Large plus small — and the premise underneath it

You want a combined book, large caps alongside small, “because small caps are not running while large caps are running”. The combination is a reasonable thing to want and I am not arguing with it. The reason given for it is the wrong way round — on the published indices and on our own closes alike — and that changes which problem we are actually solving.

What 2026 actually did

Indian market reporting for the year says the opposite of the premise. The Nifty 50 is down about 14% year to date, while the Nifty Midcap 150 is down about 2%, the Nifty Smallcap 100 is up about 8% and the Nifty Microcap 250 up about 15% — a divergence attributed to record foreign selling of roughly ₹2.8 lakh crore, which falls hardest on the large, liquid, index-weight names, as reported by Business Standard and multibagg.ai. The earnings line agrees: Nifty Smallcap 250 sales rose 11%, operating profit 22% and net profit 38% in the December quarter.

The honest counterpoint is valuation, not performance. One analysis has the Nifty 50 at or below its ten-year average multiple while the Nifty Smallcap 100 trades at 25–26 times against a 16.7 historical average, and notes that the Smallcap 250's median constituent is down about 6% since October 2024 while the index itself is up 2% — a handful of names carrying a broad, weak cohort. That is a real warning about small caps. It is not “small caps are not running”.

So the thing that is not running may be ours, not the asset class

If the broad small-cap indices are up and large caps are down, then “small caps are not running” is a statement about the small caps we picked, not about small caps. Those are different problems with different fixes. Rotating into large caps fixes the first only if large caps are where the return is — and on this page's own measurement they returned +0.4% over three years while every trading rule on them lost money. Rotating because our small-cap selection is underperforming its own index is a selection problem, and moving the same selection process onto a worse-performing asset class does not solve it.

And our own closes say the reporting is right. Measured on the same sessions, equal-weighted, with every name that priced every session: over the whole window the index members returned +15.7% against +26.9% for the rest of the universe, and over calendar 2026 +7.1% against +15.4%. Non-index names beat large caps by roughly two to one in both periods, including this year. Breadth agrees: 335 of 510 non-index names are up in 2026 against 18 of 24 index members — a similar share, so this is not a handful of outliers dragging an average.

Read these two figures against each other, not against the +0.4% benchmark higher up the page. That benchmark is 48 index names over all 715 sessions from the large-cap bar history; this cohort is drawn from the universe close series, which keeps only the 534 names that priced every session in its own aligned span — 24 index members and 510 others. Different universe, different span, so the absolute levels are not comparable. The comparison that is valid is the one being made: index against non-index, same sessions, same method.

What a combined book would need, if you want one anyway

Taking the ask at face value and setting the premise aside, a large-cap sleeve is a reasonable thing to want for a reason this page does support: drawdown. The benchmark basket of 48 index names drew down 13.1% at its worst over three years. Our small-cap book does considerably worse than that in a bad month. A sleeve that moves less is worth having even when it returns little.

But the measurement says the sleeve should not be traded. Nothing on this page found a way to buy large caps on a signal that beat buying them on no signal at all, and the benchmark — own all of them, hold — beat all 980 rules. If a large-cap sleeve goes in, the measured form of it is a held basket, rebalanced, not a scanner. That is a different kind of thing from everything else this service does, and whether we want it is your call, not a measurement.

One thing not measured here, and it is the obvious next test: whether a large-cap sleeve and the small-cap book are negatively correlated enough that holding both reduces the drawdown of the whole, rather than just averaging the two returns. That is the only form of this question with a number attached that we do not yet have, and it needs the small-cap book's own series beside the large-cap one, which this command does not currently build.

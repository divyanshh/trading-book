What the stop cost

Since the restart in October, eleven names were stopped out and every single one was a loss — ₹1.35 lakh across three accounts, with no winner to set against it. The names themselves were mostly fine: most of them trade higher now than where we sold. So the question is not whether we picked badly. It is whether we held badly. This re-runs every entry we actually took, on the real bars, changing nothing but where the stop sat.

the method

One thing varies, and it is the exit

Every row below starts at the price we really filled at, on the day we really filled, and walks the real daily bars forward. The names, the sizes and the timing are exactly what we did. The only thing that changes between columns is where the stop sat. So a difference between two columns is a difference between two stop rules and nothing else — it cannot be a difference in selection, because the selection is held fixed.

That is also the limit of it. A rule that wins here wins on our names in this tape. It says nothing about whether the entries were worth taking; that question is answered further down, against the whole market rather than our book.

All three accounts buy the same names on the same days, so each decision is counted once, at the size the largest account carried. Counting them separately would make twenty-five observations look like sixty-one without adding a single independent one.

Rupees are at the quantity actually bought in the largest account, so they understate the whole-book figure by roughly a quarter. “Still open” counts positions the rule had not exited by the last close — most of October’s entries are too recent to have run the full forty sessions, so the looser rules are carrying live mark-to-market rather than a settled result.

The win rate does not move. It is 44% in every single row, because a stop cannot turn a loser into a winner. What moves is the size of the average loss: −5.06% under the tightest rule, −3.87% under the loosest. Our stops were being hit on noise and the names were then recovering, so the tighter stop produced the bigger realised loss. That is the opposite of how a stop is supposed to behave, and it is the whole finding.

Our live rule — judged on a close, with the broker’s trigger resting beneath it — comes out at −₹35,092 against −₹11,257 for a plain 8% stop on a close. On twenty-five decisions in one account.

the thing that stings

We tested a rule, and then ran a different one

There is a committed, versioned exit policy in the service: an 8% initial stop that trails up to just under the 20-day moving average once there are enough closes to compute one. It has a backtest, a profile and three scheduled jobs wired to it. In both grids above it beats what we actually run, in every window.

Its switch reads 0. The migration that introduced it is called “execution policy disabled”, the model’s own comment notes that no live position has ever used it, and the live parameter dump confirms the value today. Everything we have placed since has used hand-chosen structural stops at a median of 5.2% instead.

So the honest statement of the problem is not “our stop is wrong”. It is: the stop we measured is not the stop we ship. The 8% rule was tested and left off; the 5% rule was shipped and never tested. Four windows now say the untested one is the worse of the two.

the cost of fixing it

A wider stop is a smaller position

The grid holds quantity constant, which is the right way to isolate the stop but not the way the book is actually sized. Position size comes from the risk budget divided by the distance to the stop, so moving from 5.2% to 8% buys roughly a third fewer shares of the same name for the same rupees at risk.

Of the twenty-one October entries in the main account, twenty were limited by the risk budget and only one by the deployment cap — so there is no cushion there. The size reduction is real and it is most of the way to a third.

That does not undo the finding, because what improves is the percentage per trade, and percentages survive the rescaling: October’s mean per decision goes from −0.23% to +0.34% on a third less capital at work. But it does mean the rupee columns above are the upper bound of the benefit, not the expected one, and it is a real decision rather than a free win.

the other half of the question

What breadth is and is not worth

The entry rule — only buy while the share of names above their 20-day average is rising against its own recent mean, and the index is above its own 20-day average — was replayed against the whole market, 268 sessions and about a thousand names, so that the answer does not depend on our twenty-five trades.

  • The rule as a whole is not measurable here. Days it permits returned +0.23 percentage points more over the next twenty sessions than days it refuses; shuffling which days it permitted beat that spread 71 times in 200. That is noise.
  • The breadth leg is the half that carries what little signal there is — about +1.0pp on disjoint windows — but it points one way in the first half of the year and the other way in the second, which is a description of a regime rather than a rule.
  • The index-above-its-average leg measures as actively harmful: −5.3pp on disjoint windows, and not marginally so. Buying while the index sits below its own average caught the rebounds; waiting for it to climb back above meant paying up for them. This is the leg that has kept us out of the market for most of November.
  • Sized into a real book, the direction is friendlier — replaying entries through the same slots and capital, days the full rule permitted returned about 9.8pp more per session than days it refused — but the confidence interval runs from −7.5 to +26 and the permutation test puts it at p = 0.065. Suggestive, not established.
  • Do not exit on breadth. Holding a working position through a falling-breadth session still returned +0.45% on average and was positive 58% of the time. Booking returns zero by construction, so booking on that signal loses money even though the spread itself is real.

Put plainly: the gate is not what went wrong, and it is not what will fix it. It is, at best, worth what it costs; the index leg of it may be worth less than nothing. The stop is where the money actually went.

what I would change

Three things, in order of how well they are evidenced

1. Turn on the rule we already tested. The 8%-plus-trail policy is written, versioned, scheduled and off. It beats the current stop in all four windows. It is not the best cell in the grid, and that is the point — the best cells are “12%” and “no stop”, which win because this sample contains no crash. The 8% rule is where the curve flattens while a real stop still exists. Switching it on costs about a third of position size at the same risk, which is the trade.

2. Stop judging the stop on an intraday wick where we still do. Close-based beats intraday in every window, by ₹8,000 on twenty-five October decisions and by ₹2.1 lakh across April to September. This one is already partly done — the close-based rule was re-armed on 23 November — and the grid says that was the right call.

3. Re-examine the index leg of the entry gate, and do not touch the breadth leg. The index leg measures negative and strongly so; the breadth leg measures positive but unstably. Neither finding is strong enough to justify trading on its inverse, but the index leg is costing us sessions on the strength of something that has never been measured as working.

What this does not support: removing the stop, widening past 8%, or sizing up. Those win in this sample because this sample has no crash in it. The average loss grows from −5.0% to −14.2% as the stop loosens, and the one thing a 2026 Indian sample cannot tell you is what that tail does in a year that has one.

what would make this wrong

The honest limits

  • Twenty-five decisions since October. Nothing can be fitted to that, which is why the same grid is shown on 102 earlier ones and split in half again. The October number is an illustration; the April–September numbers are the evidence.
  • Most October entries have not run the full forty sessions. The looser rules are still holding them, so part of their advantage is unrealised and could still reverse.
  • No crash in the sample. Every window here is 2026. A rule that wins by holding through drawdowns is flattered by a period that had no serious one.
  • The entry-gate replay cannot reproduce the full market-status machinery — it uses price proxies for it and says so. The breadth half is exact; the index half is an approximation of a richer state.
  • Survivorship. The breadth series is computed from today’s active list of names, not the list as it stood on each past day.

Why it is not working

Thirty-two positions were closed across the three accounts in November. Two of them were wins, and those two together made ₹130. The rest came back as losses totalling ₹1.47 lakh. The obvious reading is that the selection has broken — but it has not: fourteen of the twenty names we sold trade above our exit today, and eighteen of the twenty printed at least 3% above it at some point after we were out. We have been right about the stock and wrong about the moment, and we have been wrong about the moment on every name at once.

New entries are off at two levels: every account is paused, and the service itself now refuses a buy on any path, in any account, until that is deliberately reversed and redeployed. Fourteen of the twenty names with enough history are above our exit today; the median move since the sale is +4.32%. Of the eighteen we closed at a loss, twelve now trade higher than where we sold them. This is post-exit drift and not a forfeited profit — holding would have tied up the capital that funded the next entry, and that cost is not in the table. But as evidence about the exit it is unambiguous: the scanner put us in names that went up. We were not in them when they did.

Read down the index vs 20MA column. We opened almost the entire book between +6.7% and +7.6% — the most stretched the index had been in the whole run — and we did it while breadth was at its high and more than ten points above its own prior average. That is the end of a thrust, not the start of one. Buying eleven names into five sessions of it does not make eleven trades; it makes one trade in eleven tickers, and one ordinary pullback closes all of them together. That is exactly what happened: by 21 November breadth had fallen from 76% to 54% while the index itself barely moved, which is the market narrowing underneath a flat headline — and the book lives in the part that narrowed.

Nine plain stop-outs, every single one between −4.1% and −7.6%, with a median life of seven days. Not one position was given the chance to be wrong for a week and still be a trade. Put that beside section 2: these stops sat four to seven percent under entries taken while the index itself was six to seven percent above its own 20-day average. A pullback that merely returns the market to its average is a complete stop-out of the book, by construction, before the stock has done anything wrong. The names prove it — most of them turned straight back up.

SG Mart is the clean illustration and deserves naming. It was bought at 937.76 and ran to 1,248 — up 33.8%, a four-fold return on what it risked. The trail is anchored to a 20-day average, and with the stock that far ahead of its own average the trail computed below the entry price, so the stop never moved off 937.76 and not one rupee of that gain was ever protected. It was then sold on 25 November at 889.95: a loss, ₹11,604 across two accounts, on a position that had been up a third.

t-default only, which is the account with history on both sides. Nothing in the rules changed between those two months. What changed is where in the move we were buying: in September the setups were coming off bases early in the advance, in November they were coming off an index already extended, with distribution accumulating underneath. The machine is not broken. It has no sense of when in a move it is, so it buys the late part of an advance with the same conviction as the early part — and the late part is where the stops are cheapest to hit.

The honest statement of the problem: we have a selection edge and no timing edge, and the position sizing assumes the two are independent when they are not. Twelve positions opened in a week are not twelve independent bets with twelve independent stops; they are one bet on the next two weeks of the smallcap tape, sized twelve times over.

6 · What was done today, and what is still open

Done, this morning:

  • All three accounts paused, and new entries refused in code on every path — resuming is a deliberate decision and a redeploy, not a change of mood.
  • Idle cash parked in all three accounts.
  • Stops brought up to the rule everywhere they could be, and the two names where that failed are still protected at their previous level.

Open, and none of it should be decided from a losing fortnight alone:

  • The exit rule that was actually measured, over four separate windows, has still never been switched on — the shipped stop was the worst or near-worst cell in every one of those windows. The full replay is here.
  • Nothing anywhere limits how much of the book may be opened into one week, or how extended the market may be when it is opened. Both are measurable before they are made into rules, and neither should become a rule before it is measured.
  • Our own market read and MarketSmith's disagreed on five of the sessions we were buying in, and the disagreement was always the same way round: a smallcap warning the large-cap index was not carrying. Which of the two the entry gate should obey is an open question, not an oversight.
  • Two held names cannot have their stops moved at all because of a bookkeeping fault in how an added position is recorded. They are protected, but they are frozen.

Everything above is bookkeeping against the broker's fills, not a backtest and not the account's statement. Two of the thirty-two exits were booked at the recorded stop because no execution record was found for them, so any figure including those two is approximate by a few hundred rupees.

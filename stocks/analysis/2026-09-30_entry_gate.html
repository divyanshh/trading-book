Does the entry gate earn its refusals?

Tested 30 September 2026 · backtest_entry_gate and backtest_gate_replay · 543 sessions, 23 July 2024 – 30 September 2026

The question

Our gate blocks every buy unless breadth is rising against its own prior five-session mean and the market is not in a correction. It is inherited from O'Neil — "three out of four stocks follow the market" — it is enforced in code, and it was written after May 2026, a month of 61 trades, a 21% hit rate and −₹1.09 lakh.

It had also never been measured on our own data. Neither had G3, G5 or industry relative strength, and all three turned out to buy nothing.

Test one: the average forward return

Every session classified by the two legs price can settle exactly, then the forward return of the strongest third by trailing return — the names that look buyable on the days the gate refuses them. One observation per session, 462 sessions, 20-day horizon:

Day-level permutation: p = 0.871. At a 10-day horizon the picture is identical — gate −0.37pp, p = 0.861. The breadth leg is exactly zero: the days it permits and the days it refuses have the same forward return to two decimal places. Every leg flips sign between the halves.

Two methodology errors, both caught, both worth keeping

The first run reported t = −3.58 and looked decisive. Those 1,970 "disjoint samples" were about twelve sessions. Every name shares one market that day, so stock-days are not the sample size and the statistic was inflated by roughly the square root of the names. Counting sessions dropped it to noise.

Then the stock-day figures suggested "breadth rising helps, +3.38pp at t = 8.47". At the session level that is −0.01pp. Reported as a finding it would have been flatly wrong, and it is the reason both commands now print the honest unit and say why.

Test two: the real policy, with its stop

The first test measures a basket, and a basket has no stop. Our edge is asymmetric — about a third of trades win, carried by winners several times the size of the losers — and that shape depends on winners being allowed to run. A choppy tape need not lower the average at all and can still destroy the strategy by stopping positions out before the average arrives. That is the mechanism the gate is supposed to protect, and test one is blind to it.

So: enter at the next session's open, hand each candidate to domain.book_policy.replay — the same function the historical regression and live close processing use — stop at entry × 0.92, MA20 trail, twelve candidates per session because the book has twelve slots. 3,580 replayed entries.

Exit reasons are near-identical too: gate open closes on a stop 77.5% of the time and gaps through the disaster line 17.0%; gate shut, 75.6% and 17.4%. A replayed win rate of 33–37% against the real book's 32.1% is a reassuring sign the replay is not fantasy.

The stop-out rate is the whole answer, and it is 94.5% against 92.9%. The gate's protective mechanism — the one thing test one could not see — does not exist in the data. Trades entered on permitted days are stopped out marginally more often, win slightly less often, and return less on average.

Per session: gate open 138 days @ +0.89%, shut 283 days @ +3.19%, spread −2.30pp, t = −2.81. Halves: +2.31pp then −6.17pp. Permutation p = 0.945.

What I am not concluding

Every apparent effect here points against the gate, and none of it is significant once the day is the unit. That is absence of evidence, and on a question this consequential the two are worth keeping apart.

The remaining gap is the candidate list. Both tests select on trailing return — the strongest third, then the top twelve. That is a momentum proxy, not our seven-gate scanner. A real setup has a base, a pivot, a contraction stop and fundamental gates, and it is possible the market filter matters more for those than for a momentum basket. Closing that gap needs the scanner replayed over the same window, which is a larger piece of work.

The recommendation

Do not change the gate. Not because the evidence supports it — it does not, at any horizon, on either quantity — but because:

  • The measurement says nothing, and "nothing" is not a mandate to remove a rule.
  • Every spread flips sign across the window's halves, in all four tests. That is the signature of describing a regime, and this window holds one correction and one recovery.
  • The rule was written after a real ₹1.09 lakh loss whose shape — the most trades of any month, the worst hit rate — is exactly what an unfiltered tape produces.
  • It is tested against a momentum proxy, not against our actual setups.

What this does change is the confidence. The gate is not a proven edge; it is an unproven rule we keep for asymmetric reasons. It should be first in the queue when the scanner replay exists, and nobody should defend it as measured, because it is not.

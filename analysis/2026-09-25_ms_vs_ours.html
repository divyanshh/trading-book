MarketSmith's Exits Against Ours

We ship a close-based structural stop, breakeven at +8% and a 20-day trail. MarketSmith ships a fixed 7–8% loss rule and a 20–25% profit target. Both claim to be the right way to leave a trade and they had never been measured against each other on the same fills. 192 cells across six levers, replayed on the six-month book's own daily bars. The arm that matches what we ship reproduces its pinned figure of ₹21.16L exactly, which is what grounds the rest.

One finding, and it is not about MarketSmith

Every lever that takes a winner off the table early costs money on this book, and every lever that keeps you in pays. That is the whole result; the rest is detail.

Each lever is measured only on the cells that differ in it and nothing else, so every comparison is paired twice over — same trade, same other five settings. The p-values are paired sign-flip permutations over the pooled per-trade differences.

The profit target is the single worst rule tested

MarketSmith's 20–25% target costs 1.0 to 1.1 points a trade, and in not one of the sixty-four paired comparisons was it positive. The best it ever managed was −0.02pp. This is the rule we declined to port, and the decision holds.

Our own 20-day trail is the same mistake in a milder form: −0.59pp, worst case −2.59pp. Removing it from the shipped rule and changing nothing else is worth +1.57pp a trade and ₹10.1L on this book (p = 0.047).

What each named arm makes

Rupees at the book's own sizing — ₹40,000 risk, ₹5L deploy cap, quantity from each arm's own stop. Per-trade return is the primary number because it is free of sizing; the two can disagree when an arm's stops are wider.

The 8-week hold does not make money

It is the largest single effect in the table at +1.55pp — and it is an interaction, not an effect. With no target and no trail to suspend, the hold is inert: those cells are identical to four decimal places. Put a target and a trail back and it is worth +2.28pp (p = 0.0295). It does not win trades. It stops the other rules losing them.

The uncomfortable one

A flat −8% stop beats our structural stop by +0.53pp, and was never negative in any paired cell (p = 0.0001). A −7% stop beats it too, by +0.18pp. This is a finding against a rule we shipped this morning, and it is consistent with something already known about this book: fifteen of sixty stopped losers never closed below their stop, and thirteen of those were back above entry within twenty sessions. Wider stops survive noise.

Before acting on that: it is six months and 129 trades, the stop is being measured on fills that were chosen under our rules, and a sample that mean-reverts rewards width. It also cuts against the one thing the structural stop demonstrably does better — it matches the stops actually recorded in the book to a median 2.24% against 8.61% for the old pivot rule. Measure it on the population before moving it.

What this does not say

The best of 192 cells beat ours by +2.83pp at p = 0.0167. Multiplied through 192 comparisons that is 1.00. A single winning cell out of a grid this size is luck until it is shown otherwise, and 116 of the 192 beat ours, which is itself a reason to look at the levers rather than the leaderboard. The main-effect table is the evidence here; the leaderboard is not.
